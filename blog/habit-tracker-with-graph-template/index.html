Habit Tracker with Graph Template: E-ink, Paper, Notion

November 1, 2026 • 9 min read

Key takeaways

  • A habit tracker with graph template pairs a weekly checkmark grid with a trend line, so streaks, dips, and plateaus become visible instead of hidden.
  • Graphs only earn their space for quantifiable or time-based habits; binary habits are fully served by a dot grid.
  • Design printable PDFs at native device resolutions like 1620 x 2160 for the reMarkable Paper Pro and export as vector PDF with 1 pt minimum strokes.
  • Notion's chart view plots habit rollups automatically but requires a connection, so it fails offline and on e-ink tablets.
  • On paper, count 5 mm dots to size the grid, plot one dot per day against a 0-5 score, and trace a master layout into each new month.

A habit tracker with graph template pairs a weekly habit grid with a trend line so you can see streaks and dips, not just checkmarks. This guide walks you through building the same tracker three ways: a printable PDF at native e-ink resolutions, a paper notebook spread, and a Notion version with an automatic graph.

What is a habit tracker with graph template and why add a graph to a weekly grid?

A habit tracker with graph template is a single page or spread that combines two elements: a weekly grid where you mark each habit per day, and a trend graph that plots a habit's completion rate or count over time. The grid answers "did I do it today?" while the graph answers "how am I doing this month?"

What the graph adds that a grid cannot show

Checkmarks hide shape. A grid full of marks looks identical whether you completed a habit in bursts with long gaps or kept a steady rhythm, and a plateau in intensity is invisible when every day is just a filled box. A line across the same page makes streaks, dips, and flat stretches obvious at a glance, which is why tools like Notion added a chart view to databases (see Notion's chart view documentation for the database chart type). On e-ink, the same idea works in a hyperlinked PDF: grid on the left page, graph on the right, with a tab link jumping between weeks. We break down layout options for e-ink in habit tracker and planner layouts for e-ink tablets.

Which habits benefit most from graph-based tracking versus simple checkmarks?

A graph earns its page space when your habit has a number attached. Binary habits (meditate yes/no, journal yes/no) give you a streak, not a trend, so a filled dot grid tells the whole story. Quantifiable and time-based habits produce data that varies week to week, and that variation is exactly what a trend line reveals.

Habit typeExampleGraph worth it?
BinaryFlossed, journalledOptional
QuantifiablePages read, glasses of waterEssential
Time-basedDeep work minutes, workout lengthYes, as duration log

Quantifiable habits are the clear case. If you read 12, 40, 8, and 31 pages across four weeks, a checkmark hides the pattern; a line graph shows the mid-month dip instantly.

Time-based habits work well as a duration log, plotting minutes per day rather than done/not-done. On a single-page layout, put the weekly grid on the left and the graph on the right, the arrangement used in the EP Template Studio comparison of template builders. Binary habits can share the graph's row: mark the dot, and let the line above carry only the habits with numbers.

How do you design a weekly habit tracker template with a trend graph on one page?

Think of the page as two zones: a habit grid on the left or top, and a trend graph filling the remaining space. For a portrait A4-ish page at 7–10 habits, give the grid roughly 60% of the width and the graph 40%. Fewer than 5 habits, flip it: graph on top, grid below, so the eye lands on the trend first.

Grid-to-graph ratio

A weekly grid needs one column per day plus a label column, so 8 columns minimum. Keep row height at least 34 pt on a reMarkable or Supernote so pen checkmarks don't collide. The graph zone should hold 12–13 weekly points if you're trending a quarterly metric, or 7 daily points for a single week. Anything denser and the line becomes noise on e-ink's grey tones. For spacing math, a Moleskine large notebook's 5 x 8.25 inch page is a useful reference frame for how much grid a human hand comfortably fills (Moleskine product specs).

Sizing for e-ink PPI and stroke feel

Design at native resolution: reMarkable Paper Pro is 1620 x 2160, Supernote A5 X is 1404 x 1872, BOOX Go 10.3 is 2480 x 3508. Halve nothing; export vector PDF so lines stay crisp at 227 PPI or beyond. Keep hairlines at 1 pt minimum, since thinner strokes alias badly on e-ink. If your exported PDF looks soft or mis-scaled on your device, the calibration notes at https://notebook-studio.com/support.html cover the usual culprits.

Where can you find or make a printable habit tracker template with graphing space?

You have two routes: download a pre-made hyperlinked PDF, or build your own so the habit grid and trend graph match your device's exact PPI. Pre-made files are faster but rarely fit a reMarkable Paper Pro or Supernote A5 X2 Manta natively, and you inherit someone else's habit list. Building your own takes an evening and gives you a tracker you can rebuild in minutes.

How do you build a Notion template habit tracker with an automatic graph view?

Database structure

Create a database with one row per day. Add a checkbox property for each habit (Sleep by 11, Workout, Read) plus a Number property for anything you measure, like pages read. Add a second database for Weeks, and use a rollup or relation to pull each week's checkbox totals into one row. That weekly rollup is what your graph plots.

Chart view setup

On the Weeks database, add a chart view and choose Line. Set the date property as the X axis and each habit's rollup as a Y series. You get one trend line per habit, no formulas required. Notion's own chart view documentation covers the axis and series options if a setting hides from you.

What Notion costs you offline

Notion needs a connection to render charts, so your graph is dead on a reMarkable or Supernote, whose e-ink screens run at native resolutions like 229 PPI on the reMarkable Paper Pro (reMarkable). If your tracker must live on the tablet itself, a designed PDF built in Notebook Studio keeps the same weekly grid and graph layout offline; Notion wins only when automatic math matters more than page-turning. Marcus Reed covers that tradeoff in more depth at his author page.

How do you adapt a graph tracker for a habit tracker notebook template or Moleskine layout?

Paper forces you to shrink everything. A standard Moleskine large notebook gives you 70 mm of usable width per page, so plan the grid and graph before drawing anything.

Dot-grid spacing math

Moleskine dot paper uses 5 mm spacing. Count dots, not centimeters: a 7-habit weekly grid needs 8 vertical lines (7 habits plus a header row) and 9 horizontal lines (7 days plus totals). At 5 mm per dot, that is 35 mm tall and 40 mm wide, leaving the rest of the page for the graph. Use a 3 mm mini-grid inside the graph area if your habits need finer resolution.

Monthly graph on one page

Draw a 30-day line graph below the grid: x-axis is days 1–30, y-axis is a 0–5 completion score. Plot one dot per day, connect with a ruler weekly. Leave the right third blank for a monthly summary: longest streak, best weekday, worst weekday. Redraw the layout once as a master, then trace it into each new month.

How do you read your habit graph to spot streaks, dips, and plateaus?

A rising trend means the habit is consolidating. Habit formation research from Lally et al. at UCL found habits take a median 66 days to become automatic, so an upward slope in the first two months is normal, not a finish line.

Weekly oscillation, where weekdays run high and weekends dip, points to a context problem rather than a motivation problem. Anchor the habit to a weekend anchor instead of a workday one.

A plateau, a flat line after steady gains, usually means the habit has become automatic enough to stop noticing. If it still matters, raise the difficulty; if not, let the graph hold the line.

A cliff, a sudden drop to zero, signals a broken cue, not lost progress. Restart the streak without backfilling; the gap on your tracker is data, not failure.

Which medium should you pick: printable PDF, paper, or Notion?

The printable PDF wins for e-ink: it works offline, it uses your device's native PPI, and hyperlink page turning feels native on a reMarkable, Supernote, or BOOX. The cost is effort. You design the grid and graph yourself, and every data point is hand-drawn.

Paper is the lowest-effort build and never needs charging, but you do the math in your head and a week of missed entries is a page you cannot re-sort.

Notion does the graphing for you, which suits habits where the number matters more than the writing. It fails offline and on e-ink.

If your habit is binary and device-bound, build the PDF. If it is numeric and screen-bound, use Notion.

FAQ

Can I get a free printable habit tracker template with a graph already on it?

Pre-made free templates exist, but they rarely match your device's native resolution, so lines blur on e-ink screens. Building your own takes an evening: set your device's exact pixel dimensions, place a weekly grid on one side and a trend graph on the other, and export as a vector PDF. You also control the habit list instead of inheriting someone else's.

Does a habit tracker with graph work on reMarkable or Supernote without an app?

Yes. A vector PDF designed at native resolution, 1620 x 2160 for the reMarkable Paper Pro or 1404 x 1872 for the Supernote A5 X, renders directly on the device with no app. Add hyperlink zones to jump between weekly spreads and a monthly overview, and page turning feels native. The tradeoff is that the graph is a static frame you fill in by hand.

How many habits should one weekly habit tracker template with a graph hold?

Seven to ten is the practical ceiling. The grid needs one column per day plus a label column, and each row needs at least 34 pt of height so pen checkmarks don't collide on e-ink. With fewer than five habits, flip the layout and put the graph on top so the trend is the first thing your eye lands on.

What is the difference between a habit tracker notebook template and a digital one?

A paper notebook template forces you to shrink everything and do the math yourself: count 5 mm dots to size the grid, plot graph points by hand, and redraw the layout each month. A digital template handles sizing automatically at your device's resolution and can hyperlink between pages. Paper never needs charging; digital can be rebuilt in minutes rather than re-traced.

How do I make the graph fill in automatically on paper?

You can't, and that's the honest limit of paper. The closest workaround is drawing a master layout once and tracing it into each new month, then plotting one dot per day against a 0-5 completion score and connecting dots weekly with a ruler. If automatic plotting matters more than page-turning, a Notion database with a chart view does the graphing for you.
